Case study · Procurement analytics

Which suppliers can we rely on?

A manufacturer buying from 45 suppliers could not see in one place who delivers on time, who is getting worse and where it depends on a single source. I designed the data model for that question, wrote the Power Query steps and DAX measures for a Power BI report, and checked every number a second time in SQL.

Simulated data. Built on simulated data, modelled on problems from my work in program delivery. The company, its 45 suppliers and every number on this page are fictional. No data from Greenox or any other employer was used.

01The problem

Forty-five suppliers, and no single view of them.

Picture a mid-sized German maker of electronic control units (ECUs), the small computers that run functions in a car. It has two plants, in Augsburg and Chemnitz, and buys around 300 materials in six categories: semiconductors, passive components, printed circuit boards (PCBs), connectors, housings and packaging. Over 24 months it placed 5,958 purchase order lines with 45 suppliers in 11 countries.

The purchasing team asks four questions. Which suppliers deliver on time and in full? Who is getting worse? Where does the company depend on a single source? And which large suppliers still have an open sustainability risk assessment? OTIF (on time in full) is the share of order lines that arrived by the promised date and in the full quantity. Lead time is the number of days between placing an order and receiving it.

This is close to my own work. At Greenox Tech I managed procurement for the recycling platform, from sourcing parts and placing orders to tracking shipments (see that role). In a case study with SAP and Dimplex I analysed how a central data platform changes procurement reporting and buying decisions (see the case study), and in a university project with Paltech I mapped each requirement of the EU Cyber Resilience Act to its compliance status, evidence and open actions (see the project). This project is not from any of them. It uses simulated data to show how I would build the reporting.

02The data model

One fact table, four ways to slice it.

The report sits on a star schema: one table of events in the middle, called the fact table, with one row for every purchase order line, and four dimension tables around it that describe the supplier, the material, the plant and the date. Every relationship runs from one row in a dimension to many rows in the fact, and filters flow in one direction only, from the dimension into the fact. Picking a supplier filters its order lines, never the other way round, so every number has one clear meaning.

The data arrives the way an ERP system, the software a manufacturer runs purchasing and production on, would export it: order lines in one file and goods receipts, the record of what physically arrived, in another. One order line can arrive in two receipts. But every delivery measure belongs to the order line, so Power Query, the step in Power BI that shapes data before it reaches the model, sums the receipts per line and merges them in. The model ends up with one fact table, and with no relationship between two fact tables to confuse the filters.

The measures are written in DAX, Power BI's formula language. A measure is a formula that recalculates for whatever is selected on the page, such as OTIF for one supplier in one quarter. All of them are in measures.dax with a comment on what each one means, and every one is calculated a second time in SQL so the report can be checked against it.

03Where the money goes

Spend sits with a handful of suppliers.

The standard price is the price the company planned to pay for a material, and price variance is how far the price actually paid sat above or below it. The reject rate is the share of received units that failed incoming inspection, the check a factory runs on parts as they arrive. A Pareto chart sorts suppliers from the largest spend to the smallest: the bars show each supplier's share and the line adds the shares up, so you can read off how many suppliers carry most of the money. Both are percentages, so they share one axis.

04Delivery

Two suppliers are slipping.

The chart follows OTIF by the quarter in which lines were ordered. The grey line is every supplier except two; the coloured lines are the two whose OTIF fell most between the first half of 2024 and the second half of 2025. Only lines whose promised date had passed by the data cut-off count, and a due line that never arrived counts as a failure, so a late supplier cannot look better by not delivering.

05Supplier scorecard

A ranking that shows its own weights.

The score combines four parts, each from 0 to 100: delivery (OTIF), quality (100 with no rejects, falling to 0 at a 2% reject rate), price (100 at or below the standard price, falling to 0 at 10% above it) and risk (points for the invented risk tier, minus points for an open or overdue risk assessment). The sliders set how much each part counts. In Power BI they are what-if parameters, sliders that feed a number into the measures; here the same arithmetic runs in your browser.

06Risk

Where one problem would hurt most.

Two risk lenses sit side by side. The country risk tier is invented for this data set and describes logistics exposure only: Low for suppliers that deliver by truck within Europe, Medium for transatlantic, High for Asian sea freight. It is not an assessment of any country. The due-diligence status imitates the supplier risk assessments that companies covered by the German Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG) track, and says whether the latest one is done, open or overdue. Nothing here is a statement about what the act requires.

08Findings

What this data says, and what I would do next.

Three findings
  1. Spend and risk sit in the same few places. In this data set, the three largest suppliers carry 37.9% of spend, all three ship semiconductors by Asian sea freight, and two of them have open risk assessments.
  2. Two suppliers are getting worse, steadily. In this data set, Taldex Connect Kft. and Ulmont Circuits s.r.o. lost 49.6 and 41.3 percentage points of OTIF in two years, while the other suppliers held near 80%.
  3. One shortage shows how exposed the main chip is. In this data set, semiconductor lead times reached 145.5 days for orders placed in 2025 Q2, and the main microcontroller, €10.3M of spend, has only one approved supplier.
What I would recommend in purchasing
  1. Put the two slipping suppliers on a recovery plan. Agree a weekly delivery review with each, set a date to be back above 80% OTIF, and move volume to an approved second source where one exists.
  2. Qualify a second source for the main microcontroller first. Then work down the single-source list by spend, starting with the materials whose only supplier is in the High tier.
  3. Close the open and overdue risk assessments by spend. Start with the High-tier suppliers that hold €70.8M, and show assessment status on the scorecard so it is reviewed together with delivery, not separately.
09How it is built

Six pieces, all of them open.

The data, the Power Query steps, the DAX and a second calculation of every KPI in SQL are all published. The SQL runs in DuckDB, a small database that runs SQL directly on files on a laptop, with no server.

  1. simulate.pySimulates two years of purchasing with a fixed random seed and writes six CSV files shaped like an ERP extract. View ↗
  2. Power QuerySets the data types, sums goods receipts per PO line, merges them into the order lines and adds the on-time and in-full columns. Read the guide ↗
  3. measures.daxEvery measure with a plain-English comment, in display folders for spend, delivery, quality, price, risk and score. View ↗
  4. kpis.sqlThe same KPIs in SQL. A small script runs it and writes the numbers on this page and the "check your build" table in the guide. View ↗
  5. theme.jsonThe portfolio's colours as a Power BI theme, checked for colour-blind readers. View ↗
